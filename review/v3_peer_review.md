# Referee Report (Round 3): "The Gate Knows, the Threshold Doesn't: Recalibrating Activation-Gated Jailbreak Defenses Under Unseen Framings"

Venue/track: ACL/EMNLP, LLM safety and robustness. Reviewer stance: skeptical, statistics background. I read `manuscript/main.tex` in full, the tables in `manuscript/tables` (detection, oracle, robust_prereg, robust_k30, ablation, flood, second_model, breakdown, theory_check), and the round-2 review (`review/v2_peer_review.md`). I did not re-run code. Where I say "not reported", I mean not reported in the manuscript.

---

## 1. Summary

The paper studies activation-gated jailbreak defenses: probes or condition vectors on the residual stream that trigger a refusal steer. Gates are trained and thresholded on four "seen" framings, then tested on withheld and "fresh" framings. Thirteen framings are used in total, with one to three fixed templates each.

- **Diagnosis.** At the shared threshold, recall on withheld framings (fiction, indirect injection) falls to 0-17%. A user-token-mean probe still has AUROC 0.95-1.00 on framings the model can parse. A per-framing oracle threshold restores 57-81% recall. The authors conclude the failure is mostly calibration. Projecting out a framing subspace does not help.
- **Theory (new in this round).**
  - Prop. 1: under a location-scale model, AUROC is invariant and a fixed threshold only slides the operating point along a common ROC curve.
  - Prop. 2: an "impossibility" result, resting on the observation that a benign framing at location a+d and a harmful framing at location a have the same unlabeled score distribution.
  - Prop. 3: the TPR/FPR of a median/MAD-standardised rule under a Gaussian mixture, evaluated numerically (Table "breakdown").
  - Validation: the empirical version of Prop. 3 predicts oracle-grouped firing with correlation 0.99+ and MAE about 1 point (Table "theorycheck").
  - A clustering-error paragraph and a flooding analysis.
- **Method.** Cluster the stream (PCA, k-means, silhouette), standardise the score by cluster median/MAD, and fire if z exceeds z* or the shared threshold is exceeded (hybrid).
- **Results.**
  - Non-transductive halves A/B over 20 windows. With a pre-registered k_max=12 the method under-segments and gains little (fiction 5 to 19%). With a post-hoc k_max=30, fiction recall is 91%, indirect 92%, academic 84% and payload-split 90% in benign-rich traffic, at 1.6% mean FPR.
  - In "realistic" traffic, with 50% harmful prevalence assigned to attack-oriented framings, the adaptive rule collapses to 0-4% on those framings. The hybrid floor recovers 55-82% on the three seen attack framings but not on payload-split (0%).
  - Judged ASR drops (fiction 89 to 37%, code 45 to 6%).
  - A replication on Phi-3.5-mini with frozen settings reproduces the diagnosis and most of the gains, except fiction at generation level (the steer is ineffective).

The paper is considerably better than the version I reviewed before. It now has a non-transductive protocol, multiple windows with intervals for generation, ablations against k=1 and oracle grouping, a second model, an honest account of the post-hoc k_max and of the design leakage, and an explicit treatment of the attack-heavy-framing regime that I flagged as M1. The writing is candid. My concerns now concern (i) how much the new theory is worth, (ii) the circularity of its validation, (iii) the breadth of the evidence, and (iv) the thin adaptive-attack and baseline coverage.

---

## 2. Strengths

1. **Clear, well-posed question and a useful decomposition.** Separating ranking (AUROC) from operating point (threshold) in the gating setting is the right lens, and Tables "detection" and "oracle" show it cleanly.
2. **Much-improved protocol.** The halves-A/B non-transductive evaluation, 20 (or 10) windows, the five fresh framings committed before outputs were generated, and the explicit paragraph "Protocol and its departures" answer most of my round-2 M2 and M8. Disclosing that the layer was not selected and that LOFO slightly preferred layers 21/7 is the kind of detail that is rare.
3. **Honest reporting of the failure regimes.** Persona/refusal-suppression/prefix at 50% prevalence collapses to 0-4%, payload-split is lost, base64 is a capability failure, Phi fiction fails because of the intervention, and the pre-registered k_max=12 result is shown next to the post-hoc one. Table "ablation" showing k=1 does nothing (1/5/0/30/0) is informative and answers my earlier baseline request M6.1-2.
4. **A second model with frozen configuration** substantively improves on the single-model concern (M11). Showing the diagnosis (AUROC 0.98-1.00, oracle 82-90%) replicates is valuable.
5. **The flooding and contamination analysis is a genuinely useful way to characterise the method's operating envelope**, and the observation that flooding returns the gate to its uncalibrated behaviour (fiction hybrid 0.8% at pi=0.5) is an important, clearly stated limit.
6. **The authors correctly note, in the text, that the theory check "shows that contamination explains the failure and not that the method generalises."** That sentence is accurate and I give credit for it. It also limits what the validation can claim (see W3).

## 3. Weaknesses (ranked)

### W1. The theory is mostly a restatement; Proposition 2 is not an impossibility theorem in any meaningful sense (soundness of claims, high)

**Proposition 1** is a one-line observation: AUROC is invariant under a strictly increasing transform, so a location-scale family has a fixed ROC curve. This is true by definition of the model. It is a reformulation of the *assumption* ("the framing moves and stretches both classes together") rather than a consequence the data could have refuted. The nontrivial empirical content, that real framings are well described by a common ROC curve, is only indirectly tested (AUROCs are in the 0.95-1.00 range for parseable framings, but that is a statement about separability, not about a shared ROC curve across framings; for instance, no overlay of per-framing ROC curves or test of equal-shape is shown). A reader could equally interpret the data with any model where AUROC is high but the operating point moves.

**Proposition 2** is a non-identifiability example, not a theorem. It exhibits one pair of populations (all-benign at a+d, all-harmful at a) that produce identical unlabeled distributions under a Gaussian, equal-variance assumption, and concludes that no label-free rule can be "safe for every prevalence". Three problems.

- (a) The conclusion is trivially stronger than the construction needs. Any procedure that is a function of the unlabeled score distribution only cannot distinguish two scenarios with the same distribution; this is a restatement of the elementary fact that a mixture is unidentifiable without further assumptions, and it is a well-known, textbook issue in the label-shift and PU-learning literatures (Saerens et al., BBSE/MLLS identifiability conditions, class-prior estimation under "mutual irreducibility", Blanchard et al., Scott). The paper cites some of these in related work but does not situate Prop. 2 relative to them. "Price of identifiability" is exactly what that literature calls an irreducibility/anchor assumption.
- (b) The example is a *shift-by-a-known-distance* scenario. It depends on the unlabeled scores of the two populations coinciding exactly, which requires the shifted location a to be adversarially chosen. It says nothing quantitative: no minimax risk, no lower bound on regret as a function of the allowed prevalence class, no characterisation of the best rule under a prevalence bound pi <= pi_max. A real impossibility result would state the class of rules, a risk, a prior-bound assumption, and a lower bound (and ideally show the median/MAD rule is, or is not, near-optimal). The paper instead says "no choice of statistic removes it", which is a claim about all statistics that is not proved beyond the single indistinguishable pair.
- (c) It is also not clear it applies to the setting actually used. The clustered rule uses **structure beyond the unlabeled scores**, namely the activation features phi used for clustering, and the shared threshold. The proof shows score-only rules act identically. The deployed rule is not score-only. Whether a feature-based rule can break the symmetry is exactly the interesting question (e.g., the harmful and benign prompts in a framing may have different activation geometry), and the paper's own cluster-purity result (clusters are single-framing, with effective pi within 0.02) shows the clustering ignores the label. So the conclusion holds in practice, but via a different argument than the one given.

Verdict on Prop. 2: it is a correct, useful remark that motivates a prevalence assumption, and it should be labelled a remark or an observation. Calling it an "impossibility result" in the contributions list overclaims, and I would object to that wording in review.

**Proposition 3** is the evaluation of a closed-form formula. Given m and sigma defined as roots of monotone equations (the median and MAD of a two-component Gaussian mixture), TPR and FPR follow by plugging into Phi. There is no statement about monotonicity in pi, no rate, no bound, no proof of the qualitative claims ("degrades smoothly", "a stronger probe tolerates more contamination"), and no analytic characterisation of the break point. The claims are read off Table "breakdown". Some obvious analytic results are missing and would make it substantive: the breakdown prevalence as a function of d and z* (median/MAD have breakdown points of 50%, but the *operating* breakdown, where the rule's recall falls below some level, is what matters); the closed-form first-order bias of the median and the MAD in pi; a statement that the rule's FPR is *increasing/decreasing* in pi; or the optimal z* as a function of prior pi_max. As written, Prop. 3 is "a numerical table plus commentary", and the proposition label is ornamental. I would accept it as a **calculation** in a section titled "analysis", not as a proposition.

Overall: the section provides useful intuition and a plausible explanation, but the theoretical contribution is modest. The abstract's "A location-scale model explains it, shows that no label-free correction is safe without a bound on the harmful share of each framing" overstates it. The contributions bullet "an impossibility result for label-free recalibration" should be dropped or rephrased.

### W2. The model's assumptions are untested where they matter (soundness, high)

- The model has one location and one scale per framing and a common label-conditional noise distribution F_y. The text itself shows that the model's assumption fails for the clustered rule: for indirect injection the family-level model predicts 27% and the clustered rule achieves 90%, i.e. a 63-point error, which the paper reinterprets as "a source of gain, not of loss." Table "ablation" shows the oracle-grouped rule gets 29% on indirect injection against 92% for clusters. That is a large, systematic violation of the model on a headline framing, and it means the theory does **not** describe the method that is proposed. A model that predicts the oracle-grouped rule within 1 point but misses the deployed rule by 11 points on average (and by 63 on one headline framing) explains the *analysis tool*, not the *method*.
- The Gaussian-equal-variance assumptions in Props. 2-3 are never checked against the score distributions (probe logits are unlikely to be Gaussian; the shapes matter for tail FPR at 1%). The empirical version of Prop. 3 avoids Gaussianity, which is good, but then Prop. 3 as stated is not what is tested.
- The model has no account of why a framing induces a *downward* shift (rather than upward), why the shift magnitudes are what they are, or why the shift would be common to both classes. That is the substantive question and the theory assumes it away.

### W3. The validation of the theory is circular in the way the authors partially concede (soundness, high)

The check in Table "theorycheck" builds the mixture of the **empirical benign and harmful test scores** at prevalence pi, computes its median and MAD, and predicts the FPR/TPR of the oracle-grouped rule. It then compares with window measurements that are drawn **from the same test scores** (the windows resample the same test prompts, with halves A/B; the oracle grouping uses true labels of framing). The prediction is therefore a deterministic functional (median, MAD, then threshold) of the very same empirical distribution that generates the measurements, up to sampling noise from halving and window draws. A correlation of 0.998 and an MAE of 1.4 points are what one would expect from *any* correct implementation of the estimator, i.e. this is a **unit test of the code that simulates streams, not an empirical test of the model**. A model that said "scores are anything, take the empirical distribution" would score the same. The paper's note that the check "isolates the robust-statistics step" is correct but the abstract states "its predictions match measured firing rates to about one percentage point of recall", which a reader will take as evidence for the location-scale model.

What a non-circular validation would look like:
1. Fit the model on one split (validation framings, or one half) and predict a different split/framing/model. E.g., estimate (a_f, b_f) and F_y from the validation wrap of each framing and predict test-split firing.
2. Predict on **framings not used to fit z***, or predict from only **unlabeled** statistics and a prevalence assumption (as a deployer would).
3. Evaluate the model's central assumption directly: test whether F_0 and F_1 in each framing are related by the same (a_f, b_f) transform (e.g., compare quantile-quantile maps of benign vs harmful scores per framing, or fit (a_f, b_f) separately on benign and harmful and test equality).
4. Report prediction on the deployed clustered rule, not only on the oracle grouping. This is where the paper reports 0.88 correlation, MAE 11 points, errors "mostly one-sided"; that is the real validation number and it is relegated to a paragraph.

Note also that z* = 2.52 is "fitted on Qwen" (validation streams) and is applied unchanged in the Phi predictions. That cross-model use is fine, but the correlation reported for Phi is again computed on Phi's own test scores.

### W4. Two small models do not support claims at the strength made (external validity, high)

- Both models are small (1.5B, 3.8B), the gate reads a single middle layer, and the probe is a single user-mean logistic regression. Claims such as "Activation-gated jailbreak defenses fail on withheld framings mainly because their threshold stays where it was calibrated" are universal in form. Evidence is for one probe family on two models. Notably, **the diagnosis itself is not uniform across models**: on Phi the shared threshold already gets 47% on fiction, 68% academic, 74% code and 88% translation (Table "second_model"), so the "collapse to 0-17%" headline does not replicate on the second model for fiction. The abstract says "recall ... often falls to 0-17%", which holds for Qwen; for Phi the shared-threshold failure is concentrated on indirect (21%), past tense (0%) and payload-split (20%). That deserves a sentence.
- The paper's other gates (CAST, MLP, ARETE) are only evaluated on Qwen, and only the user-mean probe has AUROC near 0.99. For the other gates the "information is still there" claim is not made. The title ("The gate knows") therefore applies to one gate on two models.
- The most informative model-size question, whether larger models parse base64 and change the calibration/capability split, is explicitly deferred to Limitations. Safety-relevant models in deployment are mostly 7B+. A 7-8B model (4-bit, if needed) would be the single most persuasive generalisation check. Both models have the 96-token generation and a fixed steering strength tuned on the first.
- The Phi replication supports the claim that "frozen choices transfer" but k_max=30 was selected after seeing Qwen results on the same framings, and the same 13 framings and templates are used. It is a replication across models, not across framings, and not independent of the post-hoc choice (see W5). Seeds: it is not stated whether probe training varies across seeds.

### W5. Post-hoc k_max=30 and its interaction with the claim "with enough clusters, it works" (soundness, medium-high)

- The honest labelling is appreciated. But the pre-registered configuration (k_max=12) performs poorly: fiction 5 to 19%, indirect 16 to 31%, past tense 0 to 15%, and academic 40 to 45% (benign-rich). The headline numbers (91/92/84/90) all come from the post-hoc setting, which was chosen on the **same test framings and same templates** that report it. The paper's mitigation is that Phi used k_max=30 "without change"; but the choice was already informed by Qwen on the same 13 framings, so Phi is semi-independent.
- Recall rising monotonically in k_max (27, 70, 91 on fiction) with 13 framings built from about 25 templates strongly suggests the method is recovering the **template** (verbatim prefix/suffix strings), so as k_max approaches the number of templates the clusters become pure by template. This is the concern I raised as M3 in round 2, and the new paper acknowledges it in Limitations, but it also undermines the headline: Table "ablation" shows clustering (k=30) beats grouping by true framing family (91 vs 76 on fiction; 92 vs 29 on indirect). The paper says clusters "can also separate templates within a family". It is more natural to read this as clusters recovering exact template strings, which is a much easier problem than clustering real-world framings. With paraphrased or generated framings, there is no reason that k_max=30 would be enough, and the "silhouette-selected k" mechanism with a huge variety is untested.
- Direct framing adaptive recall **falls** from 91% (k=12) to 76% (k=30) in benign-rich traffic, and from 93 to 70% in realistic traffic. FPR on benign-rich traffic rises from 1.5 to 1.6%. That cost is hidden by the hybrid rule. The paper says the hybrid keeps almost all gains but the direct-traffic loss for the adaptive rule is noticeable and affects the most common traffic. It should be discussed. (Hybrid gets 95%, which is fine, but then the contribution of the adaptive half on the dominant framing is negative.)
- The k_max choice is not a tuned hyperparameter with a validation-based procedure. It is described as "set well above the expected number of distinct templates" in the Discussion, which is a recipe that needs knowledge of the number of templates. How does a deployer know this?

### W6. The "realistic" traffic mix is itself a design choice that determines the negative result, and benign-rich mix still favours the method (soundness, medium)

- Realistic traffic assigns 50% prevalence to six framings and 5% to seven, by fiat. The collapse in the first group (0-4%) is a direct consequence of picking 50%. At 5% those framings would be fine (Table benign-rich). The paper's conclusion ("fails where a framing is mostly harmful") is correct and trivially implied by Prop. 3, but the *mixes* are not derived from any data about real traffic. WildJailbreak or in-the-wild jailbreak corpora (adversarial-benign vs adversarial-harmful) would give empirical within-framing prevalences. Notice also that the paper's own "persona" framing has real benign use (role-play), and "refusal suppression" and "prefix injection" are indeed mostly harmful. So the grouping into dual-use vs attack-only is the authors' judgment.
- In the benign-rich mix every framing includes the **full set of benign test prompts** (225 per framing) by construction, so each cluster has a large benign support. The sensitivity to the *number of benign prompts per framing* in the window (5, 10, 30, 100) is not reported, even though the fallback threshold for small clusters is 30. For a *novel* framing, early in its appearance, the cluster would have few prompts and would fall back to validation statistics (i.e., to something like the shared rule). The relevant deployment question, how quickly does adaptation kick in, is not answered. (E1 in my previous review; partially addressed by the realistic mix, not by the support sweep.)
- The 20-draw variance: the table gives means only for recall. No intervals appear in the detection tables (only the generation table has 95% intervals). Please add them to Tables "robust" and "second_model".

### W7. Adaptive-attacker analysis is limited to flooding, and flooding is a weak and partly self-defeating attack (threat model, medium-high)

- The only adversary analysed is a uniform increase of the harmful share in one framing. The paper notes the attacker must send "about 0.43 harmful prompts for each benign one". Several points:
  - The cost is stated per framing cluster, but the attacker also needs benign prompts to *not* be in the cluster, which is cheap: the attacker controls their own prompts. A far cheaper attack is **cluster camouflage / cluster steering**: prepend or append plausible plain benign content so that the harmful prompt falls into a cluster whose median/MAD are the plain-traffic ones, which are low (the shared-threshold behaviour on plain is already 96%, so camouflage into plain would be caught!). The real attack is the opposite: **append a long framing-agnostic wrapper that moves the prompt into a small, sparse, new cluster**, which falls back to validation statistics (the shared threshold) where the probe is known to fail on withheld framings. The fallback rule for clusters under 30 prompts is thus a **designed-in attack surface**: an attacker who diversifies templates so that no cluster accumulates 30 prompts gets precisely the uncalibrated behaviour. This is trivially cheap (paraphrase each prompt) and the paper's own Discussion says "over-segmenting is cheap because small clusters fall back to validation statistics", which is the same property the attacker would exploit. This is not tested or even mentioned in Limitations.
  - Also untested: **poisoning the window** with benign-looking but high-scoring prompts, which raises the cluster median and MAD (the attacker wants to inflate sigma, not only m). Inflating MAD does not need a 0.43 ratio of *harmful* prompts at all; it requires prompts with large score variance in that cluster. The Prop. 3 analysis only considers contamination by true harmful mixtures, not by arbitrary score distributions chosen adversarially. A worst-case contamination analysis (Huber epsilon-contamination with arbitrary contaminating distribution; the median/MAD breakdown point is 50%, but the *operating-point* sensitivity is what matters) is the natural theoretical extension and would be actual theory.
  - The paper says the harmful prompts the flooder sends "are the traffic that goes through". That is not quite right: at pi=0.3 in fiction, adaptive recall is 17%, so about 83% of the flood's harmful prompts pass the gate; the attacker's per-success cost is 0.43 harmful per benign prompt, yes, but the attacker only has to maintain this ratio for a window of recent traffic, and benign prompts can be bought or synthesised. How the window rolls (freezing centroids and stats once: Algorithm line 3) matters. A frozen window is not attackable by flooding after the freeze but is stale; a rolling window is attackable. The algorithm freezes statistics, but the paper does not say how often or whether windows are refreshed. This is the key design parameter of a deployed system and defines the attack surface, and it is not specified.
- Optimization-based attacks (GCG against the gate, probe-aware attacks, Bailey et al.) are only cited. The GCG results in the appendix use suffixes optimised against the undefended model for 20 behaviours and do not include the adaptive method at all; this was flagged in my previous review as M9.

I would not demand the full adaptive-attack suite for a Findings paper, but the abstract and conclusion should not be read as "robust to attackers", and the fallback-for-sparse-clusters surface needs to be named and measured.

### W8. Novelty relative to concurrent and prior work is modest and the paper partly acknowledges it (novelty, medium)

- The calibration-versus-ranking diagnosis is anticipated by McKenzie et al., Kim & Lee (linearly separable but poorly calibrated off-distribution), Luo et al. (shift degrades threshold transfer before ranking), SALLIE (re-thresholding often suffices) and Doda (readout position). The paper now cites them as "concurrent work" and says it "confirms the pattern across gates, readout positions and framings". Fair, but the main body of the intro still lists the finding as the second of three. The delta is: (i) the gating setting with a steer, (ii) the framing-cluster view, (iii) the recalibration method. The diagnosis alone is not novel.
- The remedy is per-subpopulation unsupervised recalibration (Ziegler & Czyż) with robust standardisation (which is AdaBN-style per-domain normalisation in score space). The authors cite Ziegler & Czyż but do not run it, nor BBSE/EM-based prior-shift correction per cluster, nor any AdaBN/test-time-normalisation analogue (all raised before as M6.3 and M6.6). Table "ablation" now has k=1 and oracle groups, which is progress, but a generic per-cluster **quantile threshold** (set the threshold at the (1 - eps) quantile of scores in each cluster, which is the most obvious label-free method) and a **per-cluster z using mean/std** are not included. Since the robust-z rule is just "estimate a cluster's location/scale by a robust estimator", the ablation showing robust vs non-robust vs quantile per cluster would show what the MAD adds. This is cheap.
- Farzam et al. (FrameShield) is now cited in related work, but only as "likewise treats framing as a nuisance". It is still not compared empirically. The same applies to JBShield and an input-guard gate (Llama-Guard / Qwen3Guard in prompt mode), which would show whether the whole activation-gate setting is justified relative to a trained classifier. Qwen3Guard is already in the pipeline as a judge; as a gate it would cost almost nothing and also bear on the judge-gate coupling question. This was E6; it is not done.
- The theory is, as discussed, thin as a contribution on its own. Overall novelty is incremental (engineering plus diagnostic) with a good empirical characterisation; fine for Findings, not for main conference without more.

### W9. Evaluation and statistics (rigor, medium)

- FPR budgets: mean benign FPR rises from 0.6% (shared) to 1.5-1.6% (adaptive/hybrid) against a 1% budget, in the benign-rich mix. The abstract says "1.3-1.6% mean false positives", honest. But the FPR is averaged over framings; per-framing FPR (e.g., plain and base64 in the first review, 2.7% and 4.9%) and FPR on the dominant traffic are not tabulated here. With 225 benign prompts per framing, a 1.6% FPR is about 3-4 prompts; the intervals are not given.
- The over-refusal reading: 60.3% to 60.6% on plain benign. With a 60% undefended refusal rate (the 1.5B model on XSTest/OR-Bench) this metric is saturated and nearly uninformative about harm to benign users. Phi's 23.9 to 24.8% is more informative. Over-refusal on wrapped benign prompts is not reported (judge confusion), but wrapped benign prompts are exactly where the adaptive rule changes behaviour (FPR 1.5% vs 0.6%). A cheap alternative: report the fraction of benign wrapped prompts that fire (already known: FPR), plus a downstream helpfulness measure for those that fire.
- Generation-level ASR is "composed" from always-on and undefended responses under greedy decoding. That is valid if the gate decision is made before generation and the steer is applied at all positions of the prompt and response, but the gate decision depends on the prompt only, so the composition is exact. Fine. However the judge is one model (Qwen3Guard-4B) with no second judge or human audit; the judge's artefact on base64 is acknowledged, and I would add a validation sample for fiction (a narrative that hedges can be mislabelled).
- Steering strength is fixed with no sweep, and on Phi the steer does nothing for fiction. Hence ASR gains conflate gate recall and steer efficacy, and the paper acknowledges this for Phi but not as a general confound.
- Statistical tests for the claimed differences, e.g. shared vs hybrid ASR, are not given; intervals in the generation table exist but the detection tables have none.

### W10. Smaller issues

- Abstract: "nine of them withheld from design" but the Setup describes four withheld from the start and five fresh framings written after the main study; the five fresh ones were designed by the same authors after seeing results on the first four, with the same one-to-three-template generation process. They are withheld from the *method's configuration*, not from the authors' prior. Calling them "pre-registered" is acceptable only if the commit hash and timestamp are given in the paper (a reviewer cannot verify). Please give them.
- The "location-scale" model in Prop. 1 has b_f>0 but the paper never reports the fitted (a_f, b_f) per framing. A scatter plot of fitted a_f against framing, with benign and harmful fits, would be the single most informative figure for this section. There are no figures in the paper at all.
- "its predictions match measured firing rates to about one percentage point of recall": see W3.
- "AUROC 0.95-1.00 whenever the model can parse the framing": the condition "can parse" is determined post hoc by the AUROC being high, which is circular. Define parseability independently (e.g., the model's own comprehension accuracy on the framed prompt, or a ground-truth decoding test for base64/leetspeak).
- Prop. 3's statement "for d>=2 and z*=2.52 ... recall is below 0.3% at pi=1/2": Table "breakdown" shows 0.2 for d=2 and 0.0 for the others, consistent, but the sentence "at d=4 recall is 90% at pi=0.05 and 13% at pi=0.30" agrees with the table. Good. Note that for the d values observed in practice (AUROC 0.99 corresponds to d about 3.3), the model predicts 49-60% recall at pi=0.05-0.10, yet the measured oracle-grouped recall is 76-90%. Please explain how Gaussian d maps to the real probe distributions (heavy tails and non-unit variance matter).
- Format: the file is `\documentclass{article}` with `\usepackage[review]{acl}`, which is fine; Limitations and Ethics sections are present now.
- Table "ablation" is reported for 5 of 13 framings only, with FPR averaged over 13.

## 4. Questions to the authors

1. Prop. 2: can you state a quantitative minimax or lower-bound result for the class of rules that know only that pi <= pi_max, with a risk you define? Does the clustered rule, which uses features as well as scores, fall inside the class covered by the proof? If not, what, exactly, is claimed to be impossible?
2. Prop. 3: what is proved beyond numerical evaluation? Is FPR monotone in pi? Can you give the break-even prevalence as a closed form (or bound) in d and z*? Can you extend to Huber contamination with arbitrary (adversarial) contaminating distribution?
3. Table "theorycheck": the predictions use the empirical test benign and harmful score distributions and the window measurements resample the same test scores. What is the predicted-versus-measured error if (a_f, b_f) and F_y are estimated on validation and the prediction is made for test? What does the model predict for the deployed clustered rule on a per-framing basis, and how does that compare to the 11-point MAE and the 63-point miss on indirect injection?
4. Is the common-ROC assumption of Prop. 1 supported? Please show per-framing ROC curves or the quantile-quantile map between benign and harmful scores in each framing.
5. How does the method behave when each framing has, say, 100 or 500 distinct templates (paraphrases or generated variants)? Are the clusters still template-pure, and is k_max=30 still adequate? Can you run the benchmark with WildJailbreak or a paraphrase-expanded version of at least the withheld framings?
6. What happens to a harmful prompt that falls in a cluster with fewer than 30 prompts? In the realistic mixes, what fraction of harmful test prompts are handled by the fallback, and what is their recall? Could an attacker intentionally diversify prompts to land in such clusters?
7. How is the window maintained in deployment (size, refresh interval, whether statistics are frozen), and how does recall change if the window is rolled and partly composed of attacker traffic?
8. Why did direct-framing adaptive recall decrease from 91 to 76% when k_max went from 12 to 30 in benign-rich traffic (93 to 70% in realistic)? Is the plain cluster being split by benign content in a way that raises its thresholds?
9. How was the dual-use versus attack-only assignment (5% versus 50%) chosen, and how sensitive are conclusions to it? Could you derive within-framing prevalence from a labelled in-the-wild corpus?
