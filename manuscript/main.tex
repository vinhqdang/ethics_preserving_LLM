\title{The Gate Knows, the Threshold Doesn't:\\Recalibrating Activation-Gated Jailbreak Defenses Under Unseen Framings}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Activation-gated defenses read a model's residual stream and steer towards refusal only when a probe fires. Their thresholds are set on jailbreak framings known at development time. We test six such gates on real harmful and benign prompts in thirteen framings, nine of them withheld from design. On withheld framings, recall at a 1\% false-positive budget often falls to 0--17\%. Yet a probe over the user's tokens still ranks harmful above benign prompts with AUROC 0.96--1.00 whenever the model can parse the framing, and re-thresholding per framing would recover most of the loss. The failure is largely one of calibration. A location-scale model accounts for the shift, shows why a correction that sees only one framing's unlabeled scores needs a bound on its harmful share, and describes how recalibration degrades as that share grows. We propose a recalibration that needs no labels from the deployment stream: cluster prompts by framing, standardise the probe score robustly within each cluster, and keep the original threshold as a floor. With enough clusters, a post-hoc choice, recall on most withheld framings rises to 60--94\% on four models from three families at 0.5--1.6\% mean false positives, and judged attack success falls, for example from 89\% to 37\% on fiction framings (Qwen2.5-1.5B), where the shared threshold changes nothing. The gains do not carry over where the steering itself is ineffective (fiction on Phi-3.5-mini), and attack-only framings are not helped.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Safety training teaches a language model to refuse harmful requests. Jailbreaks undo it by changing how the request is framed: a persona, a story, a retrieved document, an encoding, or a request to write code \citep{wei2023jailbroken,shen2024dan,greshake2023indirect,li2023deepinception}. Because the framing changes the surface of a prompt but not what it asks for, a natural defense looks beneath the surface. Residual-stream activations carry a linearly readable harmfulness signal \citep{zou2023repe,zhao2025harmfulness}, and refusal itself is mediated by a single direction \citep{arditi2024refusal}. Several defenses therefore read activations to decide whether to intervene. Conditional activation steering adds a refusal direction only when a condition vector fires \citep{lee2025cast}, and probe-based detectors flag a prompt from its internal state \citep{zhang2025jbshield,mckenzie2025highstakes}. These gates are cheap, they leave benign traffic alone, and their operating point can be set to a fixed false-positive budget.

This paper asks what happens to that operating point when the framing changes. We separate framing families into \emph{seen} ones, used for training and calibration, and \emph{withheld} ones, and we evaluate on real prompts from five public benchmarks \citep{mazeika2024harmbench,zou2023gcg,chao2024jailbreakbench,rottger2024xstest,cui2025orbench}, with generations judged by a guard model \citep{qwen2025qwen3guard}.

First, every gate collapses on withheld framings. Each one detects at most 17\% of harmful prompts on withheld fiction and indirect-injection framings, and none of them changes the 89\% attack success rate of fiction prompts. This includes a geometric gate of our own design that explicitly tries to factor framing out of the representation.

The second finding concerns the cause. Ranking survives the shift. A probe that reads the mean of the user's tokens separates harmful from benign prompts with AUROC 0.96--1.00 on every withheld framing the model can parse. On the four framings the shared threshold misses worst, re-thresholding it on the new framing recovers 57--81\% recall. What moves is the location of both score distributions. Concurrent work reports compatible observations: linear jailbreak probes are poorly calibrated off-distribution \citep{kim2026linearly}, and shift degrades threshold transfer before ranking \citep{luo2026measuring}. We confirm the pattern across gates, readout positions and framings, and Section~\ref{sec:theory} gives a location-scale model that accounts for it. We also show that projecting out a subspace of framing directions, the most direct representational fix we could build, does not repair it.

The third finding is a remedy and a map of its limits. Deployed gates see a stream of mostly benign prompts. We cluster the stream by framing, standardise the probe score inside each cluster with median and median absolute deviation, which tolerate a modest share of harmful prompts \citep{rousseeuw1993mad,leys2013mad}, and keep the original threshold as a floor. Unsupervised recalibration of subpopulations has antecedents in general machine learning \citep{ziegler2019unsupervised}. Our contribution is to adapt it to activation-gated safety and to test it under a protocol that withholds framings from design.

Concretely, we contribute:
\begin{itemize}
\item a held-out-framing evaluation of activation gates on real corpora, with leave-one-framing-out selection on validation data and five framings pre-registered after the main study;
\item evidence that gate failure on withheld framings is mostly a calibration shift, a location-scale model that explains it, a remark on why label-free recalibration needs a bound on the harmful share, and a negative result for framing-subspace projection;
\item a label-free, cluster-wise recalibration with a fixed-threshold floor, evaluated without transduction at detection and generation level;
\item a characterisation of its failure modes: cluster resolution, framings used mainly for attacks, harmful prevalence, and framings the model cannot parse.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Jailbreak framings.}
Attacks range from optimised suffixes \citep{zou2023gcg,liu2024autodan} and attacker models \citep{chao2025pair,mehrotra2024tap} to hand-written templates: personas \citep{shen2024dan}, nested fiction \citep{li2023deepinception}, encodings and ciphers \citep{wei2023jailbroken,yuan2024cipherchat}, many-shot demonstrations \citep{anil2024manyshot}, and instructions planted in retrieved content \citep{greshake2023indirect}. \citet{wei2023jailbroken} attribute their success partly to mismatched generalisation, which is what a withheld framing probes.

\paragraph{Representation-level defenses.}
Harmfulness and refusal are encoded separately, with harmfulness concentrated at the instruction tokens rather than at the final position \citep{zhao2025harmfulness}. Representation engineering reads and edits such directions \citep{zou2023repe}; circuit breakers reroute harmful representations during training \citep{zou2024circuitbreakers}; latent adversarial training hardens them \citep{sheshadri2024lat}. Inference-time gates include conditional steering \citep{lee2025cast} and detectors that flag prompts from internal signals \citep{zhang2025jbshield,hu2024gradientcuff,xie2024gradsafe}, or redact during generation \citep{mei2026hiddenguard}. FrameShield likewise treats framing as a nuisance to be disentangled from the goal in activation space \citep{farzam2026hiding}. Several studies question how far probe signals generalise. Probes for high-stakes interactions keep high AUROC out of distribution while recall at low false-positive rates is modest \citep{mckenzie2025highstakes}. Final-token probes miss jailbreaks whose evidence sits in earlier user tokens \citep{doda2026beforelast}. Linear jailbreak probes conflate benign register with intent, and their headline accuracy depends on the threshold \citep{kim2026linearly}. In contrast, \citet{schwarz2026entanglement} find that refitting thresholds does not help on topic-matched pairs, where the discriminative information is absent. Internal harmfulness scores can even anti-rank successful jailbreaks \citep{luo2026measuring}. Adaptive attacks that optimise against the probe can drive recall to zero \citep{bailey2026obfuscated}. We study the complementary, non-adaptive failure of natural framing shift.

\paragraph{Guard models and judges.}
Classifier guards moderate inputs and outputs \citep{inan2023llamaguard,han2024wildguard,zeng2024shieldgemma,padhi2025graniteguardian,qwen2025qwen3guard}, and constitutional classifiers are trained against universal jailbreaks \citep{sharma2025constitutional}. We use a guard as the evaluation judge, because refusal-string matching counts harmless non-compliance as a successful attack \citep{souly2024strongreject}.

\paragraph{Calibration under shift.}
Label-shift corrections re-estimate class priors from unlabeled target data \citep{saerens2002adjusting,lipton2018bbse,alexandari2020labelshift}. Test-time adaptation adjusts normalisation layers by entropy minimisation \citep{wang2021tent}. Conformal methods restore coverage under shift given weights or labels \citep{tibshirani2019conformalshift,podkopaev2021labelshiftconformal}. Unsupervised recalibration adjusts outputs per subpopulation without labels \citep{ziegler2019unsupervised}; multicalibration asks for calibration on every identifiable subgroup, using labels \citep{hebertjohnson2018multicalibration}. For jailbreak detectors, \citet{piet2025jailbreaksovertime} adapt text classifiers to drift by self-training, \citet{azov2026sallie} find that re-thresholding often suffices when target labels exist, and \citet{kim2026linearly} combine register erasure with distribution-free calibration on labelled benign data. Our method uses no labels from the shifted framing or the deployment stream, and it operates per framing cluster of an activation gate.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Prompts.}
Harmful behaviours come from JailbreakBench, the standard text behaviours of HarmBench, and AdvBench; benign prompts come from the benign split of JailbreakBench, the safe prompts of XSTest, and 400 prompts of OR-Bench-Hard, which are designed to look harmful. After de-duplication there are 1{,}550 prompts. We split them 50/20/30 into train, validation and test \emph{by behaviour}, stratified by source, so that no wrapped version of a test request is seen in training. The test split holds 241 harmful and 225 benign requests.

\paragraph{Framings.}
Each request is wrapped in a framing that keeps its text verbatim. Four framings are \emph{seen}: the plain request, a persona (AIM, DAN, ``grandma''), refusal suppression, and prefix injection. Four were \emph{withheld} from the start: fiction or screenplay, indirect injection disguised as a retrieved document or e-mail, base64, and leetspeak. After the main study we wrote and committed five \emph{fresh} framings before generating any output on them: a past-tense historical frame, an academic or IRB frame, code completion, translate-then-answer, and payload splitting. Every family uses one to three fixed templates. We also optimise GCG suffixes \citep{zou2023gcg} against the undefended model for 20 test behaviours (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}).

\paragraph{Model and gates.}
The target is Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct \citep{qwen2024qwen25} without a system prompt. All gates read layer 14 of 28. When a gate fires, it adds the same vector $\alpha\hat r$ to the residual stream at every position. Here $\hat r$ is the normalised difference between mean harmful and mean benign activations of plain training requests, and $\alpha$ is 0.4 times the mean activation norm. Gates therefore differ only in their decision. We compare six gates: (i)~a CAST-style condition vector \citep{lee2025cast}; (ii)~an MLP probe on the final token; (iii)~ARETE, a geometric gate of our own that maps activations onto intent and context hyperspheres and steers orthogonally to a framing subspace (Appendix~\ref{app:arete}); and logistic-regression probes on (iv)~the final token, (v)~the mean of the user's tokens, and (vi)~a union of three readouts (Section~\ref{sec:diagnosis}). Every gate is trained on seen framings of the training split. Its threshold is the 99th percentile of its score on benign validation prompts of seen framings, a held-out-quantile calibration in the spirit of \citet{kim2026linearly} but without their register erasure. We call it the \emph{shared} threshold.

\paragraph{Protocol and its departures.}
Readout position, clustering layer and clustering features were selected by leave-one-framing-out (LOFO) on the validation split: each seen wrapper in turn is treated as withheld, and recall is measured on it. Two departures need stating. First, the diagnostic analyses of Section~\ref{sec:diagnosis} used the test split before the remedy was designed, so the original withheld framings informed the idea, though not its configuration. This is why we pre-registered the fresh framings. Second, the layer was fixed at 14 for all gates and never selected; in LOFO the user-mean probe scored slightly higher at layers 21 and 7 (43\% and 41\% recall) than at layer 14 (38\%).

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Detection is reported as true-positive rate (TPR, which we call recall) on harmful prompts and false-positive rate (FPR) on benign prompts, plus AUROC. Responses are generated greedily for 96 new tokens and judged by Qwen3Guard-Gen-4B in response-moderation mode \citep{qwen2025qwen3guard}. The judge sees the \emph{unwrapped} request and the response. Attack success rate (ASR) is the fraction of harmful requests whose response is judged \textsc{Unsafe}. Over-refusal is the fraction of plain benign requests whose response is judged a refusal; on wrapped benign prompts the judge often labels confused non-compliance as refusal, so we do not report it there. For base64 the judge labels responses that merely echo or start decoding the payload as \textsc{Unsafe}, so we report detection only. With 96 tokens, ASR mostly measures the onset of compliance; Appendix~\ref{app:extra} repeats the key cells at 256 tokens.

\paragraph{Projecting out framing does not help.}
ARETE assumes that framing occupies a low-dimensional subspace that can be removed. We test this in LOFO on validation data: we estimate the subspace from wrapper displacements of the remaining seen wrappers and project it out before training a linear probe. Removing 0, 4, 16 or 64 directions gives mean held-out recall of 54\%, 54\%, 54\% and 48\%. A subspace learned from two wrappers does not contain the shift induced by a third. Prefix injection is the clearest case, at about 1\% recall for every rank.

\paragraph{Where to read.}
Readout position matters more than projection. In LOFO, the final-token probe misses prefix injection (1\%), whose injected instruction follows the request, while the user-mean probe catches 60\% of it. A union of three readouts raises LOFO recall from 54\% to 69\% at 1.3\% FPR but does not help withheld framings, because each readout suffers the same shift.

\section{A Model of the Shift}
\label{sec:theory}

Why does a framing break the threshold but spare the ranking? A first-order answer is that it moves the whole score distribution and leaves the shape of each class alone. We state this as a model so that its consequences can be checked. Let $s=a_f+b_f\,\xi$ be the probe score of a prompt in framing $f$, where $\xi\sim F_y$ depends only on the label $y$, $F_1$ stochastically dominates $F_0$, and $(a_f,b_f)$, $b_f>0$, are a location and scale set by the framing. The model is an approximation: measured AUROC varies from 0.96 to 1.00 across framings the model can parse, and is near chance on base64.

\paragraph{Proposition 1 (ranking is invariant, thresholds are not).}
Within a framing, AUROC does not depend on $(a_f,b_f)$, because the map $\xi\mapsto a_f+b_f\xi$ is increasing. A fixed threshold $\tau$ yields $\mathrm{TPR}_f=1-F_1((\tau-a_f)/b_f)$ and $\mathrm{FPR}_f=1-F_0((\tau-a_f)/b_f)$. Under the model the ROC curve is the same for every framing, and the framing only slides the operating point along it. A downward shift of $a_f$ therefore lowers recall and false positives together, which is the pattern in Table~\ref{tab:detection}: benign FPR on withheld framings falls to nearly zero at the same time as recall.

\paragraph{Remark 2 (a bound on the harmful share is necessary).}
Let $F_0=\mathcal N(0,1)$ and $F_1=\mathcal N(d,1)$. A framing that is entirely benign at location $a+d$ and one that is entirely harmful at location $a$ produce the same unlabeled score distribution, $\mathcal N(a+d,1)$. A rule that sees only the unlabeled scores of one framing fires at the same rate $r$ on both populations. Its false-positive rate on the benign framing is then equal to its recall on the harmful one, so no such rule can be good on both. Recalibrating a framing from its own unlabeled scores therefore needs an assumption on its harmful share $\pi_f$. This is the assumption of Section~\ref{sec:method}: it is necessary for rules of this kind, and it is not removed by choosing a different statistic. The remark does not cover rules that use side information, such as a threshold calibrated on plain requests, which the hybrid rule of Section~\ref{sec:method} retains.

\paragraph{Proposition 3 (what contamination costs the robust statistics).}
Suppose a framing is observed as the mixture $(1-\pi)F_0+\pi F_1$, with $F_0,F_1$ unit-variance Gaussians separated by $d$. Let $m(\pi,d)$ be the median and $\sigma(\pi,d)=1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}$ of the mixture, each the root of a monotone equation. The rule that fires when $(s-m)/\sigma>z^\star$ has
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{FPR}&=1-\Phi\bigl(m+z^\star\sigma\bigr),\\
\mathrm{TPR}&=1-\Phi\bigl(m+z^\star\sigma-d\bigr).
\end{align*}
At $\pi=0$ this gives $m=0$ and $\sigma=1$, so $z^\star$ approximately attains the intended false-positive rate. As $\pi$ grows, harmful prompts pull $m$ upward and inflate $\sigma$, which raises the effective threshold. At $\pi=1/2$ the median sits between the modes, and for $d\ge2$ and $z^\star=2.52$ the threshold lies above the harmful mode, so recall is below $0.3\%$ (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}, Table~\ref{tab:breakdown}). Recall falls steeply between $\pi=0.1$ and $\pi=0.3$ at every separation. A larger $d$ raises recall at small $\pi$ but moves the breakdown only modestly: the share at which recall drops below 5\% rises from 0.24 at $d=2$ to 0.36 at $d=5$. At $d=4$, recall is 90\% at $\pi=0.05$ and 13\% at $\pi=0.30$.

\paragraph{Checking the predictions.}
We test Proposition~3 without the Gaussian assumption. For every framing and traffic mix, we build the mixture of the empirical benign and harmful test scores at the scenario's prevalence, compute its median and MAD, and predict the FPR and TPR of the rule that standardises by true framing (the oracle grouping, which removes clustering error). We compare these predictions with the window measurements of Section~\ref{sec:results}, over all thirteen framings and both mixes. Predictions and measurements agree to within 1--2 points of recall (Table~\ref{tab:theorycheck}), and the model puts persona, refusal suppression, prefix injection and payload splitting at 0\% recall at $\pi=0.5$, as measured. The table is partly circular, because the predictions are built from the same test scores that the windows resample. Predicting from one random half and measuring on the other gives the same correlation (0.999) and error (1.0 points) over 200 splits. The check therefore isolates the robust-statistics step and shows that contamination accounts for the failure. It does not show that the model predicts scores on unseen traffic, and it does not test the assumption of a common location-scale transform, which Section~\ref{sec:results} tests only indirectly through the clustered rule.

\paragraph{Clustering error.}
The oracle grouping is not what runs in deployment. Clusters hold single framings and keep their nominal harmful share, but treating each framing as one location-scale family predicts the clustered rule only roughly (correlation 0.88, mean error 11 points). The largest miss is indirect injection, predicted at 27\% and measured at 90\%: a cluster can be tighter than its framing, so its MAD is smaller and its threshold lower (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}).
\paragraph{Flooding.}
Proposition~3 also says how an attacker can degrade the rule. A rule that standardises within a cluster can be pushed off its operating point by raising the harmful share of that cluster. We let the share of one framing grow from 5\% to 50\% while the rest of the stream stays unchanged, on Qwen2.5-1.5B only (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}, Table~\ref{tab:flood}). For fiction, the oracle-grouped rule falls from 76\% recall to 0\% as predicted (Table~\ref{tab:flood}), and the clustered rule from 87\% at $\pi=0.05$ to 17\% at $\pi=0.3$. To reach that point the attacker must send about 0.43 harmful prompts for each benign one in the framing, and every harmful prompt it adds to get there goes undetected. The hybrid floor limits the damage only where the shared threshold already works, as for code (69\% recall at $\pi=0.5$, against 83\% for the shared rule at full budget). Where the shared threshold fails, as for fiction, flooding leaves little more than the uncalibrated behaviour.

\section{Cluster-Wise Robust Recalibration}
\label{sec:method}

The diagnosis and the model of Section~\ref{sec:theory} suggest treating each framing as its own calibration problem. At deployment the framing of a new prompt is unknown and no labels are available. Prompts that share a framing share an activation signature, so they cluster, and when a framing is used mostly for benign purposes the bulk of its scores describes benign prompts. By Remark~2 this assumption cannot be dropped.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Cluster-wise robust recalibration}
\label{alg:recal}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require probe score $s$; framing features $\phi$; budget $\epsilon$; shared threshold $\tau_{\epsilon/2}$
\State \textbf{Once, on validation:} simulate streams of seen framings, run lines 2--3, and set $z^\star$ to the $(1-\epsilon/2)$ quantile of benign robust $z$.
\State \textbf{Window:} on unlabeled traffic, reduce $\phi$ by PCA and run $k$-means, $k\le k_{\max}$ chosen by silhouette.
\State For each cluster $c$: $m_c\gets\mathrm{median}(s)$, $\sigma_c\gets1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}(s)$; freeze centroids and $(m_c,\sigma_c)$.
\State \textbf{New prompt} $x$, nearest centroid $c$: fire iff $s(x)>\tau_{\epsilon/2}$ or $(s(x)-m_c)/\sigma_c>z^\star$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:recal} gives the \emph{hybrid} rule. The score $s$ is the user-mean probe. The features $\phi$ are user-mean activations at layer 14, reduced to 32 principal components. Clusters with fewer than 30 prompts fall back to validation statistics. Inside a cluster the median and MAD describe its benign bulk, so a harmful prompt is compared with benign prompts of its own framing. Because $z^\star$ is set on validation streams that pass through the same clustering, it accounts for within-cluster dispersion. The \emph{adaptive} rule is the second disjunct alone, at the full budget. Validation labels are used once, to set $z^\star$ and $\tau$. No labels from the deployment stream or from new framings are used.

LOFO on validation streams at 5\% harmful prevalence selected the user-mean readout over the final-token and last-user-token readouts, and layer-14 clustering features over layers 7 and 21. Under that selection, held-out recall rose from 38\% with the shared threshold to 91\% with the adaptive rule, at 1.1\% FPR. The hybrid rule reached 83\% at 0.9\% FPR. We fixed $k_{\max}=12$ without selection. Neither number carried over to the test split: with this configuration fiction recall is 19\% (Section~\ref{sec:results}), because twelve clusters are too few for thirteen framings, which LOFO over three seen wrappers could not reveal.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluate without transduction. In each of 20 windows the test behaviours are split at random into halves A and B. Cluster statistics are estimated on traffic built from half A and applied to half B. We use two traffic mixes. \emph{Benign-rich} traffic gives every framing 5\% harmful prevalence. \emph{Realistic} traffic keeps 5\% for dual-use framings (plain, fiction, indirect, past tense, academic, code, translation) and uses 50\% for \emph{attack-only} framings, which are rarely used benignly (persona, refusal suppression, prefix injection, base64, leetspeak, payload split). Generation-level ASR is composed from judged responses. A fired prompt takes the label of its steered response, which equals the always-on response under greedy decoding, and an unfired prompt takes the label of its undefended response.

\paragraph{As pre-registered, the method under-segments.}
Table~\ref{tab:robust}a reports the frozen configuration on all thirteen framings. In benign-rich traffic, payload-split recall rises from 0\% to 90\%, indirect injection from 16\% to 31\%, past tense from 0\% to 15\%, and academic from 40\% to 45\%. Fiction gains little, from 5\% to 19\%. When the stream contains only the eight original framings, the same configuration detects 82\% of fiction and 56\% of indirect prompts. With thirteen framings and at most twelve clusters, fiction shares a cluster with framings whose benign scores sit elsewhere, and its location is not corrected. With true framing labels as groups (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) fiction recall is 76\%, so the loss comes from cluster resolution, not from the statistics.

\paragraph{With enough clusters it works where traffic is mostly benign.}
Raising $k_{\max}$ to 30 is a post-hoc change, motivated by the observation above, and we report it as such. In benign-rich traffic, adaptive recall then reaches 91\% on fiction, 92\% on indirect injection, 84\% on academic and 90\% on payload splitting, at 1.6\% mean FPR (Table~\ref{tab:robust}b). Over five seeds these four stay within 72--94\% at 1.6--2.5\% FPR, and 47--81\% with templates paraphrased by another model (shared: 0--18\%) (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}). Fiction recall rises steadily with $k_{\max}$: 27\%, 70\% and 91\% at 12, 20 and 30 in a 10-window sweep. Past tense stays at 40\%, below its 57\% oracle. In Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, one global robust $z$ ($k{=}1$) does nothing, and grouping by true framing family helps less than data-driven clusters, which can also separate templates within a family. Two checks rule out simpler explanations (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}). The clusters follow framing, not harmfulness: 99.6\% of prompts share their cluster's majority framing, no cluster of 30 or more holds over 9\% harmful prompts, and clustering on features with the probe direction removed, or on layer 7, changes recall by at most 3 points. Nor is the gain a looser budget: a shared threshold set to the hybrid rule's mean FPR finds 12\% of fiction, 33\% of indirect and 2\% of payload-split prompts.

\paragraph{An input guard works at a different operating point.}
Qwen3Guard-Gen-4B on the wrapped prompts flags 86--99\% of harmful prompts outside the two encodings, but also 16--63\% of the benign ones. At a 1\% FPR it is weaker than the probe (AUROC 0.93--0.97): thresholded on seen framings it recalls 0--7\% on withheld ones, and an oracle per-framing threshold 17--60\% (Table~\ref{tab:guard}).

\paragraph{It fails where a framing is mostly harmful, and the floor contains the damage.}
Under realistic traffic the persona, refusal-suppression and prefix-injection clusters are half harmful. Their median then sits among harmful scores, and adaptive recall on them drops to 0--4\% (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). This is worse than the shared threshold, and prefix-injection ASR returns to the undefended 77\%. Payload splitting, an attack-only framing that the shared threshold also misses, is lost entirely. The hybrid rule keeps the shared threshold as a floor and recovers 55--82\% recall on seen attack-only framings, while keeping nearly all of the gain on dual-use framings. Correcting each cluster's prior \citep{saerens2002adjusting,lipton2018bbse} fails the other way, finding 94--97\% on these attack-heavy clusters but 0--1\% of fiction and indirect injection, since a framing moves $p(x\mid y)$, not only the prior (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}).

\paragraph{A second model.}
We ran the whole pipeline on Phi-3.5-mini-instruct (3.8B parameters; \citealp{abdin2024phi3}), with every choice frozen from the Qwen study: the user-mean probe at the middle layer (16 of 32), the same clustering, budgets and steering strength (0.4 times the mean activation norm). The diagnosis replicates (Table~\ref{tab:second}). The probe's AUROC is 0.98--1.00 on the seven framings listed; the shared threshold finds 0--88\% of harmful prompts and the oracle threshold 82--90\%. The hybrid rule with $k_{\max}{=}30$ finds 61--93\% at 1.3\% mean FPR (0.3\% for the shared threshold). With 12 clusters the gain is smaller, as on Qwen. The failure on attack-heavy framings also replicates: under realistic traffic adaptive recall on persona and refusal suppression is 0\%, and the hybrid floor recovers 63--65\%.
Judged ASR falls on most framings under benign-rich traffic (past tense 19\% to 7\%, code 15\% to 8\%, payload splitting 15\% to 3\%), but fiction is an informative failure: the hybrid rule fires on 88\% of harmful fiction prompts, yet ASR stays at 38\%, because on Phi the steer at this strength does not change fiction behaviour even when always on (36.9\% undefended, 37.3\% steered).

\paragraph{A larger model and a third family.}
On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (frozen configuration, middle layer 14 of 28, Table~\ref{tab:third}) the shared threshold finds 0--17\% on fiction, indirect injection, past tense, academic and payload splitting (AUROC 0.94--1.00), the hybrid rule 57--87\% at 0.7\% FPR, and ASR on fiction falls from 44\% to 13\%. On Gemma-4-E4B-it \citep{gemma2026gemma4} (layer 21 of 42, Table~\ref{tab:gemma}) the shared threshold finds 0\% of indirect, past-tense and payload-split prompts at AUROC 0.98--1.00; the hybrid rule finds 88\%, 13\% and 61\%, and 66--77\% of fiction, academic and code (shared 33--44\%), at 0.5\% FPR.

\section{Discussion}

By AUROC, activation gates generalise to new framings; at a deployed threshold they do not. This agrees with observations that probes keep their ranking but lose their operating point off-distribution \citep{mckenzie2025highstakes,kim2026linearly,azov2026sallie}; for base64 the model never forms the request, so no threshold helps.
The remedy needs a bound on each cluster's harmful share (Section~\ref{sec:theory}), which holds for dual-use framings such as fiction, retrieved documents, academic or code requests, the hardest to block wholesale. It fails for attack-only framings, and an attacker can break it by flooding a cluster or by sending a framing at low volume; those cases need a fixed floor or a labelled check.

\section{Conclusion}
Activation-gated defenses fail on withheld framings mainly because their threshold stays put while the model still represents the harm. Recalibrating per framing cluster from unlabeled traffic, with the original threshold as a floor, recovers much of the lost detection on four models from three families and much of the lost protection for dual-use framings. It cannot protect attack-only, flooded or low-volume framings, nor fix an ineffective intervention.

\section*{Limitations}
We study four models from three families, with 1.5B to 7B parameters; the other three were run with the configuration frozen from Qwen2.5-1.5B, and larger models were not tested. Base64 stays undetectable on Phi, the 7B model and Gemma (AUROC 0.39--0.65); larger models that parse such encodings could turn that framing from a capability failure into a calibration failure. The steering strength was fixed from the first model and does not transfer to every framing: on the second model it leaves fiction unchanged. Each framing uses one to three fixed templates. Paraphrasing them costs the hybrid rule up to 43 points of recall on fiction, persona and payload splitting, and we did not test framings written by an attacker. The best-performing configuration ($k_{\max}{=}30$) was chosen after seeing pre-registered results on the first model, and the original withheld framings informed the method's design. The cleaner evidence is the fresh framings under the pre-registered configuration, and the other models, where $k_{\max}{=}30$ was applied without change. We tested one adaptive strategy, flooding a single cluster, and only on the first model. We did not test attackers who optimise against the gate \citep{bailey2026obfuscated}, or poison the window that sets the statistics. A framing sent at low volume stays in clusters below the minimum size and falls back to the validation statistics. We tested this: once at least 15 harmful prompts are sent, recall on fiction, indirect and past tense drops below 5\% (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}), so a low-volume attack removes the gain on those framings, and only the hybrid floor protects code and part of academic. The split-half check of Section~\ref{sec:theory} still resamples one test pool, so its agreement with measurements does not show that the model predicts unseen traffic, and it describes the clustered rule only roughly. ASR is judged on 96-token responses by one model judge, and generation-level ASR is composed from undefended and always-on responses. The traffic mixes are simulated; real prevalence per framing is unknown.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
This work studies defenses against misuse of language models. Harmful prompts come from public benchmarks, the attack templates are publicly documented, and generated responses are kept out of the released artefacts. We report failure modes, including attack-only framings and cluster under-segmentation, so that defenders do not over-trust activation gates.

\section{ARETE Geometry}
\label{app:arete}
ARETE standardises activations and maps them through two learned linear heads onto unit hyperspheres for intent and context. It is trained with (i)~an invariance loss pulling the intent coordinates of a wrapped prompt towards those of its plain version, (ii)~a framing-classification loss on the context sphere, and (iii)~a cap loss placing harmful intents inside, and benign intents outside, eight unsupervised spherical caps. The gate score is the maximum cap margin. Steering uses the refusal direction projected onto the orthogonal complement of a rank-16 framing subspace, estimated by SVD of displacements $h_{\text{wrapped}}-h_{\text{plain}}$ on training behaviours.

\paragraph{Cluster composition and a matched-FPR baseline.}
On Qwen2.5-1.5B with $k_{\max}{=}30$ and benign-rich traffic (10 windows), windows form 23--24 clusters on average and 99.6\% of prompts carry their cluster's majority framing. The largest harmful share of any cluster of at least 30 prompts is 9\% at 5\% prevalence, and no harmful prompt sits in a cluster whose harmful share exceeds four times the prevalence, so the clusters do not isolate harmful prompts. Projecting the probe's weight vector and the harmful-minus-benign mean difference out of the clustering features, or clustering on layer 7 instead of layer 14, changes hybrid recall on every framing by at most 3 points in both traffic mixes. The mean benign FPR hides a spread: per framing, the hybrid rule's FPR is 0.6--1.1\% on fiction, indirect, past tense, academic and translation, 2--3\% on the plain request, code and payload splitting, and 4.1\% on base64 (6.5\% under realistic traffic), whose responses the judge cannot score. \paragraph{Seed variation.}
The numbers in Table~\ref{tab:robust} come from one probe and one clustering seed. We rerun the $k_{\max}{=}30$ configuration on Qwen2.5-1.5B with five seeds; each seed fits the probe on a bootstrap resample of the training prompts (the regularised fit itself is deterministic), sets the PCA, $k$-means and silhouette seeds both for $z^*$ and in every window, and draws its own 10 A/B windows. Under benign-rich traffic, hybrid recall across seeds is 83--93\% on fiction (mean 88\%), 86--94\% on indirect injection (89\%), 72--85\% on academic (79\%), 85--92\% on payload splitting (88\%) and 36--55\% on past tense (43\%); the shared threshold reaches 2--4\%, 4--19\%, 28--43\%, 0\% and 0--1\% on the same framings. Mean benign FPR over the 13 framings is 1.6--2.5\%, and per framing at most 5.1\% outside base64 (payload splitting). Under realistic traffic the persona and refusal-suppression rows are the least stable (hybrid recall 39--72\% and 40--70\%), and payload splitting stays at 0\% in every seed. The conclusions of Section~\ref{sec:results} hold in every seed.

\paragraph{Label-shift baselines.}
Label-shift correction assumes that $p(x\mid y)$ is fixed and only the class prior changes. We apply it per cluster, on the same $k_{\max}{=}30$ clusters and windows as Table~\ref{tab:robust}b. The probe logit is Platt-scaled on validation prompts of the seen framings; in each cluster of at least 30 prompts we estimate the harmful prior $\hat\pi_c$ from the window, either by EM on the calibrated posteriors \citep{saerens2002adjusting} or by inverting the cluster's firing rate under the shared threshold with that threshold's validation TPR and FPR (BBSE, \citealp{lipton2018bbse}), and add $\operatorname{logit}\hat\pi_c-\operatorname{logit}\pi_s$ to the log-odds. Smaller clusters use the window-wide estimate. The firing threshold on corrected log-odds is set like $z^*$, on clustered validation streams of the seen framings at a 1\% budget. Table~\ref{tab:labelshift} shows the two corrections behave alike and mirror the robust $z$. Where a framing hides among benign prompts, its harmful prompts look benign to the calibrated probe, so the estimated prior is low and the correction raises the bar: fiction, indirect injection, past tense and payload splitting go to 0--1\%, at or below the shared threshold. Where a cluster is half harmful, the prior estimate is high and recall on persona, refusal suppression and prefix injection reaches 94--97\%, exactly where the robust $z$ fails. With the shared threshold as a floor, EM keeps 79--93\% on those three framings under realistic traffic but still finds at most 3\% of fiction and indirect injection. The two corrections are complementary, and a rule that chooses between them per cluster is a natural next step that we have not tested.

\paragraph{Low-volume attack and a mean/std baseline.}
We also let a target framing contribute only $n$ harmful prompts and no benign ones to the statistics window, so that its clusters stay small. On the first model the adaptive rule then recalls at most 5\% of fiction, indirect and past-tense prompts once $n\ge 15$, and falls from 78\% at $n{=}5$ to 1\% at $n{=}120$ on academic and from 60\% to 2\% on code. The hybrid floor keeps code at 70--76\% for $n\ge 15$ and academic at 26--82\%, and cannot help the other three. Replacing the median and MAD by the cluster mean and standard deviation, with a threshold set on the window's benign prompts (which uses labels and favours the baseline), gives similar recall in the base setting: 81\% against 89\% on fiction, 83\% against 90\% on indirect, equal on academic and code, and 61\% against 38\% on past tense. The gain therefore comes from standardising per cluster, and we do not claim that the robust estimator is what matters; we did not run the baseline under flooding, where the robust estimator should degrade more slowly.
